**Coast FIRE** means you already have enough invested that, if you never added another contribution, growth alone would carry the portfolio to your full retirement target by the age you chose. You still have to earn enough to pay today's bills. You have only stopped saving for retirement. The number you need today, the **Coast FIRE number**, is your target divided by the growth factor between now and then:

> Coast number = FIRE number ÷ (1 + r)<sup>n</sup>

Here `r` is the assumed yearly return after inflation and `n` is the number of years until you plan to retire. Everything useful about Coast FIRE, and everything that can go wrong with it, is in those two inputs.

## The formula, term by term

Take the three pieces in turn.

- **FIRE number.** The portfolio you want on the day you stop working. A common shortcut is annual spending divided by a withdrawal rate; the [companion article on the 25x rule](/blog/what-is-your-fire-number/) explains where that comes from and where it fails. Coast FIRE does not care how you chose it. It takes it as given.
- **Real return `r`.** The yearly growth after inflation, with returns reinvested and compounded once a year. Using a real return keeps everything in today's money, so the FIRE number can be stated in today's money too. Mixing a nominal return with a today's-money target is the most common way to get this calculation wrong.
- **Years `n`.** Whole years from now to the retirement age you picked. The portfolio is assumed to receive no new contributions during this time and no withdrawals.

## A fully worked example

The inputs, all in one currency and in today's money:

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Yearly spending in retirement | 40,000 |
| Withdrawal rate used for the target | 4% (a multiple of 25) |
| FIRE number | 40,000 × 25 = 1,000,000 |
| Age now / target retirement age | 30 / 60, so `n` = 30 |
| Real return `r` | 5% a year, compounded annually |

The growth factor is 1.05<sup>30</sup> = 4.3219 (rounded to four places). So:

> Coast number = 1,000,000 ÷ 4.3219 = **231,377**

Check it by running the clock forwards: 231,377 × 4.3219 = 1,000,000. A 30-year-old with 231,377 invested, earning exactly 5% real every year and adding nothing, reaches the target at 60.

The same sum answers a second question: when do you reach coast status if you are still saving? Say you are 35, hold 100,000, add 30,000 at the end of each year, and the target is 1,000,000 at 60 with `r` = 5%. At 43 you hold 434,219 and the coast number for that age is 436,297, so you are just short. At 44 you hold 485,930 against a coast number of 458,112, so you have crossed the line. Each year's balance is the previous one multiplied by 1.05 plus 30,000; each year's coast number is 1,000,000 ÷ 1.05<sup>(60 − age)</sup>. The [Coast FIRE calculator](/tools/coast-fire-calculator/) runs both questions with your own numbers.

## How much the return assumption matters

A coast number is highly sensitive to `r`, because the return compounds over decades. This table gives the coast number for a 1,000,000 target.

| Real return | 35 years to go | 30 years | 25 years | 20 years | 15 years |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3% | 355,383 | 411,987 | 477,606 | 553,676 | 641,862 |
| 4% | 253,415 | 308,319 | 375,117 | 456,387 | 555,265 |
| 5% | 181,290 | 231,377 | 295,303 | 376,889 | 481,017 |
| 6% | 130,105 | 174,110 | 232,999 | 311,805 | 417,265 |
| 7% | 93,663 | 131,367 | 184,249 | 258,419 | 362,446 |

Read along the 30-year column. Moving the assumed return from 5% to 3% raises the number you need from 231,377 to 411,987, a rise of 78%. Moving it to 7% cuts it to 131,367. Nothing about your finances has changed; only the assumption has.

The sensitivity also runs forwards. If you set aside 231,377 because you assumed 5% and the portfolio earns a steady 3% real, then after 30 years it is worth 231,377 × 1.03<sup>30</sup> = 561,614, about 56% of the target. At a steady 7% it is worth 1,761,304. The coast number is a point estimate of a range.

All figures here are deterministic: one constant return every year. Real markets do not deliver a constant return, which is the subject of the section on sequence risk below.

## What the simple formula leaves out

The one-line version is a good first filter and a poor plan. These are the main things it ignores.

**Living costs between now and retirement.** Coasting means earning enough to cover spending without saving. If your income would not cover your costs without drawing on the portfolio, the coast number no longer holds.

**Taxes.** The formula grows a pre-tax pot to a pre-tax target. Money taken out of many accounts is taxed. If an effective tax rate of 15% applied to withdrawals, 40,000 of after-tax spending would need about 47,059 gross, and a 25× target would move from 1,000,000 to about 1,176,471. That 15% is an arbitrary illustration, not a typical rate; real tax depends on country, account type, other income and the year.

**Contribution limits and locked accounts.** Tax-advantaged accounts usually limit how much goes in each year and when money can come out. The UK, for example, has a pension annual allowance (the [gov.uk page](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-your-private-pension/annual-allowance) gives the current amount, a tapered allowance for very high earners, and the carry-forward of unused allowance from the previous three tax years). A coast calculation that treats all your money as one freely accessible pot can mislead if much of it sits in accounts with an access age.

**Sequence risk.** Two portfolios with the same average return can end in different places if good and bad years come in a different order. During the coasting years you are not drawing money, so order matters less than in retirement, but it still matters: a deep fall in the last five years before retirement leaves little time to recover, and the first years of withdrawal then start from a smaller base. The [article on how long money lasts](/blog/how-long-will-my-money-last/) works through a computed example.

**State pensions.** A state pension that starts at a known age lowers the amount the portfolio must supply. In the UK, for example, the [new State Pension page on gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension/what-youll-get) says you need 35 qualifying years for the full rate. To see how large the effect can be, assume spending of 40,000 from age 60 to 90, a 3% real return, withdrawals at the start of each year, and a state pension of 12,000 starting at 67. The portfolio that exactly funds spending is 807,538 without the pension and 642,283 with it, 20% lower. (This is a present-value comparison, not a safe-withdrawal calculation, so the 25× number is not comparable; the point is the relative size.) The retire-at-60 plan must still pay for the seven years before the pension starts.

**Horizon.** A 25× target is built around a roughly 30-year retirement. Retiring at 50 or 55 may mean a retirement longer than that, and the target should rise. See the [FIRE number article](/blog/what-is-your-fire-number/).

## How a full projection treats these

A year-by-year projection replaces each shortcut with a calculation. It takes spending, income and each account's balance every year. It applies tax to the withdrawals actually taken. It respects contribution limits and the age at which each account can be touched. It starts state pensions at the age you set. It runs the whole plan against many sequences of returns, not one constant return, and reports how often the plan stays funded.

## Reproduce the numbers

Every figure above comes from one of two formulae. The coast number is `F / (1 + r) ** n`. The savings balance after `k` years of end-of-year contributions `c` from a starting balance `S` is `S * (1 + r) ** k + c * ((1 + r) ** k - 1) / r`. In the 35-year-old example, `k` = 9 gives 100,000 × 1.05<sup>9</sup> + 30,000 × 11.0266 = 155,133 + 330,797 = 485,930.
