If you live in the US with savings in dollars, a pension in euros from years working in Europe and a rental
flat in pounds, a planner that quietly converts everything at today's rate is hiding the biggest assumption in
the plan. Glideworth lets each item keep its own
currency, makes the exchange rate an explicit assumption, and lets you test what happens when it moves.

## How it works

- **Accounts, real assets, debts and income and expense events** can each carry a currency other than the
  household's. The household has a base currency and a display currency and locale for formatting, independent of
  the tax country.
- **Each foreign currency needs an exchange-rate pair**, in either direction, stored with the household's current
  finances. A rate is expressed as base units per one foreign unit as of a date, with an optional **annual drift**
  that moves it each year. A plan can override a pair or add drift in its own assumptions.
- **The engine reports in the base currency** and keeps the original amount alongside, so you can see both.
- **Progress snapshots, check-in net worth and the totals** the API reports convert foreign items with these
  rates. A missing pair counts at 1 and is reported; the engine itself refuses to run a plan with a missing pair.
- **Tax is worked out in the tax country's currency,** using your rates, so a plan with items in other currencies
  needs a rate for every currency involved. The audit lists any that are missing.

## Exchange-rate risk in simulations

By default exchange rates follow their drift in every trial. Give a currency pair a yearly volatility
(about 8 to 10% is typical for a major pair) and [Monte Carlo](/features/monte-carlo/) draws correlated exchange-rate
shocks alongside the market data. The setting is off by default and you switch it on per plan, so a plan that
holds one currency is never affected.

## A worked illustration

An illustrative US household has $400,000 in a brokerage account and a pension of 50,000 euros a year, from
years working in Germany, that starts at 67. Its base currency is the US dollar, and the plan assumes 1.08 dollars
per euro with no drift:

| Item | Currency | Plan figure | In base currency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US brokerage account | USD | 400,000 | $400,000 |
| Euro pension (annual) | EUR | 50,000 | $54,000 |

Now give the pair a 9% yearly volatility. The pension's value in dollars varies from trial to trial, and the
chance of success moves with it. The difference between the two runs is the exchange-rate risk in the plan, which
a fixed rate cannot show. All numbers here are invented.

## Over the API and MCP

```bash
curl -s -H "Authorization: Bearer $KEY" \
  "$GW/api/v1/households/hh-sample/fx-rates"

curl -s -X POST \
  "$GW/api/v1/households/hh-sample/fx-rates" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $KEY" \
  -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -d '{"from":"EUR","to":"USD",
       "rate":1.08,"annualDrift":0}'
```

The MCP tools are `list_fx_rates`, and, with writes enabled, `set_fx_rate` and `unset_fx_rate`. Writes honour
the household's version, so a stale edit is refused.

## What it does not do

- **No live exchange rates.** Glideworth does not fetch rates from anywhere; you enter them (the dirham's
  US-dollar peg is pre-filled). A self-hosted server makes no network calls to find one.
- **No currency hedging, forward contracts or foreign-exchange costs** on conversions.
- **Exchange-rate risk is optional and simplified:** per-pair volatility and an optional correlation between
  pairs, not a full currency model. By default rates follow their drift.
