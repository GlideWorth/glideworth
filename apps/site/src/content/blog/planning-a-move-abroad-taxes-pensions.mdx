When you move to another country, three separate questions change. Which country counts you as a **tax resident**, and from when? Which country may tax each of your incomes and assets, given that you may now live in one and still own things in the other? And what happens to your pensions, tax-free accounts and benefits? This article explains the concepts and uses a move from the United Kingdom to the United Arab Emirates (UAE) as the running example. The UK rules quoted here were read on the official gov.uk pages on 16 November 2026, and each is linked so you can check it against the current page. Several of them are complicated, and where that is the case the article says so and points to the official source, not a simplified summary.

## Residence versus source

Most countries tax in two ways, and a move changes how both apply to you.

- **Residence taxation.** A country taxes people who are resident there on their worldwide income and, in many cases, gains. Change your residence and you change which country holds this claim.
- **Source taxation.** A country may tax income that arises inside it, whoever receives it: rent from a property there, a local salary, sometimes a pension paid from there. This claim stays with the country where the income arises, even after you leave.

If both countries claim the same income, a **double tax treaty** between them divides the rights, and the country of residence usually gives a credit or exemption for tax paid at source. The gov.uk page on [tax on your UK income if you live abroad](https://www.gov.uk/tax-uk-income-live-abroad) puts the starting point plainly: "You usually have to pay tax on your UK income even if you're not a UK resident", and it lists pensions, rental income, savings interest and wages as examples. It adds that where the country you live in has a treaty with the UK, you may be able to claim UK tax relief to avoid paying tax twice.

## Residence is a test, not a choice

You do not become non-resident by announcing it. Each country applies its own rules, and both sets of rules may apply to you.

**The UK.** The UK uses the Statutory Residence Test, introduced in 2013. The gov.uk page on [residence for foreign income](https://www.gov.uk/tax-foreign-income/residence) says whether you are UK resident usually depends on how many days you spend in the UK in the tax year, and that one of the automatic UK tests is spending 183 or more days in the UK in the tax year. It is only one test: the gov.uk page lists several automatic UK tests, there are also automatic overseas tests, and a "sufficient ties" test applies where none of the automatic tests settles the question. HMRC's guidance, [RDR3: Statutory Residence Test](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rdr3-statutory-residence-test-srt), explains them in full, and the residence page links to an HMRC [residence status checker](https://www.tax.service.gov.uk/guidance/check-your-uk-residence-status). The UK tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April, so a move partway through a calendar year is judged by the tax year in which it falls.

**The UAE.** The UK-UAE treaty text defines a UAE resident as an individual who is domiciled there or has a habitual abode or centre of vital interests there. If you qualify as resident in both countries under their own rules, Article 4 of the treaty applies tie-breakers in order: the state where you have a permanent home available; if you have one in both, the state where your personal and economic relations are closer; then habitual abode; then nationality; and if those do not settle it, the two tax authorities decide by mutual agreement.

The practical point is that residence needs to be confirmed for the countries concerned, using their tests and your own facts. A planner can follow a timeline you give it, but it cannot tell you where you are resident.

## Split years

When you move in or out of the UK, gov.uk says the tax year is "usually split into 2", a non-resident part and a resident part. Split-year treatment has conditions. The same page says you will not get it in some situations, including some where you live abroad for less than a full tax year, and it refers to HMRC guidance on the detail. The treatment is not automatic and not simple; the RDR3 guidance above covers the cases.

Planning software usually approximates this. Glideworth, for one, splits a year by months, and its [documentation](/docs/planning/moving-country/) says that this is a simplification and not the UK's own split-year rules.

## What typically stays taxable in the country you leave

For a UK leaver the main groups are these.

**UK-source income.** As above, UK-source pensions, rents, interest and wages can stay within UK tax. Gov.uk says that as a non-resident you are eligible for a UK Personal Allowance if, for example, you are a British citizen, a citizen of an EEA country, or have worked for the UK government that year, or if the double-tax treaty gives it, and that you claim it after the tax year, using form R43. If you are not eligible, tax applies to all UK income from the first pound.

**Gains realised soon after leaving.** The UK has "temporary non-residence" rules. HMRC's helpsheet [HS278](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/temporary-non-residents-and-capital-gains-tax-hs278-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs278-temporary-non-residents-and-capital-gains-tax-2023) says they apply where you were UK resident for all or part of at least 4 of the 7 tax years before departure and the period you are not UK resident does not exceed 5 years. In that case certain gains, and certain income items such as some pension lump sums and withdrawals, are treated as arising in the year you return. Its list is long and has exceptions, so it is a starting point only. The temporary-non-residence guidance is also part of the RDR3 publication, which was last updated on 11 June 2026.

**Inheritance tax.** From 6 April 2025 the UK test is whether you have been resident for at least 10 of the previous 20 tax years. HMRC's [Inheritance Tax manual (IHTM47020)](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm47020) gives the years you stay in scope after leaving:

| Years of UK residence in the previous 20 | Years in scope after leaving |
| ---: | ---: |
| 13 or fewer | 3 |
| 14 | 4 |
| 15 | 5 |
| 16 | 6 |
| 17 | 7 |
| 18 | 8 |
| 19 | 9 |
| 20 | 10 |

The manual also has transitional provisions for some people, which this table does not cover.

**Tax-free accounts.** Under the gov.uk guidance on [ISAs if you move abroad](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/if-you-move-abroad), once you are non-UK resident you cannot pay into an ISA, you must tell your provider, and you keep UK tax relief on what is already in it. Other countries may not recognise a foreign tax-free wrapper at all, so the receiving country's rules matter.

## Treaty relief, in concept

A treaty works like a rulebook for each type of income. The rule can give the right to tax to the country of residence, to the source country, or to both with a credit. For the UK-UAE convention, the text of [Article 17](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5da850dce5274a5ca94bb5cc/2016_UK-UAE_Double_Taxation_Convention___in_force.pdf) says that, subject to the rule on government service, pensions and similar payments to a resident of one state are taxable only in that state. Government-service pensions have their own rule in Article 18. In outline, then, a private pension paid to someone who is a UAE resident under the treaty is taxable in the UAE, which the UAE government's [taxation page](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/finance-and-investment/taxation) says does not levy income tax on individuals. That page also lists VAT at 5%, excise tax and corporate tax, so "no income tax" is not "no tax".

Three cautions. Relief is something that has to be claimed and evidenced, not something that applies by itself. HMRC also publishes summary tables of treaties in its [Double Taxation Relief Manual](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/double-taxation-relief/dt19752), which itself says the treaty text should be consulted for full detail, and a summary table should be read against that text. And treaty rules do not cover everything, such as UK property gains or income from a UK employment.

## The UK State Pension abroad

The UK pays the State Pension worldwide, but the yearly increase depends on where you live. The gov.uk page on [State Pension rates abroad](https://www.gov.uk/state-pension-if-you-retire-abroad/rates-of-state-pension) says it will only increase each year if you live in the European Economic Area, Gibraltar, Switzerland or a country with a social security agreement with the UK, and not in Canada or New Zealand even though both have agreements. It says you will not get yearly increases if you live outside those places, and that the pension goes up to the current rate if you return to live in the UK. The [list of countries where an annual increase is paid](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-pensions-annual-increases-if-you-live-abroad/countries-where-we-pay-an-annual-increase-in-the-state-pension) names 50 countries and territories. The UAE is not among them on the date this was checked.

The pension is therefore frozen in cash terms while you live there. To see what that does, suppose prices rise 2.5% a year (an assumption, not a forecast). The buying power of a frozen amount is `1 ÷ 1.025^years`:

| Years frozen | Buying power left |
| ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 78.1% |
| 15 | 69.0% |
| 20 | 61.0% |
| 25 | 53.9% |

At 2% inflation the 10-, 20- and 30-year figures are 82.0%, 67.3% and 55.2%. A plan that assumes the State Pension rises with inflation will overstate it for a leaver in such a country.

## How a plan can model a move

A useful plan has to carry the following: a timeline of where each person lives and when; a country attached to each account, property and income; the source-tax rules for non-residents; the relevant treaty; the State Pension freeze; and the exit and return rules.
