**Before you start:** a plan, and your household's country set in [Current finances](/docs/planning/build-a-plan/).

<Callout type="warning" title="Estimates, not tax returns">
  Every tax figure is an estimate from stated tax-year tables and simplified rules. It is not a tax return and not
  advice. No country's preset has been checked against every official calculator.
</Callout>

## Choose the tax mode

In the **Plan editor**, open **Taxes**. The tax mode has three choices:

- **Estimate for my location**: country rules for income tax, social contributions, allowances, gains and
  dividends. This is the one to use if your country is covered.
- **Flat rates**: a single rate per kind of income. Simple, and crude.
- **My own brackets**: your own bands, and a wealth tax if you need one.

New households start on estimate whenever their country has a preset. Other options here include the filing status
(for the US and similar), "Future thresholds" (as legislated then with inflation, rise with inflation every
year, or frozen forever), and whether tax is paid in the same period or settled the next.

## Countries covered

The engine has a national tax model for {facts.taxCountryCount} countries: {facts.taxCountries.map((c) => c.name).join(', ')}.
The US model covers federal, payroll, every state and DC, and some cities. Canada covers federal and
province, and Spain covers the state and region. Each country has a [planning page](/planning/) listing
what it models, what it leaves out and the tax years in the tables.

Tax years in the tables, as of 2026-11-16: UK 2025/26, 2026/27 and announced changes for 2027/28 and 2029/30; US
federal, payroll and states 2026; Canada 2026; Australia 2025-26 to 2027-28; Germany, Netherlands, Israel and
Ireland 2026; New Zealand 2026-27; France the 2026 finance law with 2026 social rates; Spain the 2025 income-tax
scales with 2026 social security. Later years carry the last known rates forward and index thresholds with
inflation, which is wrong whenever the rules change.

Germany, the Netherlands, France, Spain, Israel, Ireland and New Zealand are estimated at a basic level. The
UK, US, Canada and Australia leave things out too. The whole list is shown with your tax results, and the summary
is on [Known limitations](/docs/methodology/known-limitations/).

## Statutory rules, whatever the mode

For every country covered, limits come from the account's country regardless of tax mode: contribution limits,
pension access ages, home-sale exclusions and minimum distributions.

## Several currencies

Accounts, real assets, debts and events can be in another currency from your household's.

<Steps>
1. Set the item's **Country** or currency. The app offers to switch the currency when the country implies one.
2. Add an exchange rate: the rate is base-currency units per one foreign unit, as of the finances date, with an
   optional yearly drift. One pair works in either direction. A plan can override a pair.
3. Read results in the household currency. The original-currency detail is kept.
</Steps>

A missing rate is a problem: the engine refuses to run a plan that needs it, and the audit lists missing pairs.
In the chance-of-success settings, "Exchange-rate risk" lets a foreign currency vary with a yearly volatility
(about 8 to 10 percent is typical). By default exchange rates follow their drift.

## Assets and income in another country

The household lives in one country for the whole plan: the one set under **Tax residence** in Current finances.
Accounts, property, debts and income can sit in another country, and the tax estimate follows them there.

- **Where each thing is.** Every account, property, debt and income has a country. It defaults to the household's
  country, or the account type's (an ISA is British).
- **Which country may tax what.** Your country of residence taxes worldwide income. The country where something is
  may also tax it as a non-resident (rent and property gains almost everywhere). A treaty table says who keeps the
  right to tax each kind of income, and your country of residence gives a credit for tax paid elsewhere.
- **Wrappers abroad.** Your country of residence may not recognise a foreign tax-free account; by default its growth
  is taxed on withdrawal. You cannot pay into an ISA, a UK pension, a TFSA or an RRSP while you live outside its
  country.
- **Citizenship.** In Current finances, open **About you**, then **Citizenship**, to add citizenships or a US green
  card. US citizens and green-card holders also pay US federal tax wherever they live, after the foreign earned
  income exclusion and a credit.

In the **Taxes** results, a plan with anything abroad shows tax by country and by reason (residence, source,
citizenship, relief), and "How this is taxed, and why" explains any item with its source.

<Callout type="note" title="Moving country is not modelled">
  There is no change of residence during a plan, so no departure tax, no State Pension frozen abroad and no change
  in UK inheritance tax scope. Only UK-UAE is implemented article by article; other treaty pairs are basic or use
  the credit method with a warning. See [Known limitations](/docs/methodology/known-limitations/).
</Callout>

## What this does not do

- It does not apply every local rule. Gains are realised pro rata to cost basis and treated as long-term, and
  losses are never realised, so there is no loss relief.
- It does not know future law. Anything not yet legislated is your assumption.
- It does not model Corporate Tax or VAT (UAE) or means-tested benefits (UK Universal Credit, Pension Credit).
