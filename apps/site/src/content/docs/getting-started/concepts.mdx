Every screen in the app is built from a few ideas. Learn these and the rest follows.

## Household

The people you plan for: one person, or two for a couple. Each has a birth month. The household also
holds where you live (one country for the whole plan) and the currency. Dependents (children, people you support) are separate from the
people.

## Current finances

Where you are today: accounts (cash, 401(k)s, IRAs, ISAs, pensions, investments), real assets (a home, a car, a rental)
and debts, each with an owner. These are balances, not a forecast. You update them by hand, by CSV
import (a bank statement saved as CSV works), or from a script, and every plan that starts "now" reads them. An update can record
a **progress snapshot**, with at most one automatic snapshot per day.

## Plans

A plan is a scenario built on your finances: milestones, income, expenses, savings flows, assumptions,
tax settings and withdrawal rules. You can have many plans (retire at 60, retire at 57, downsize at 70).
A plan keeps a **version history**: every change is a new version you can go back to. A **branch** is a
plan that stores only its differences from a parent and follows the parent when it changes.

## Milestones

Named moments the plan hangs things on, such as "Retirement", "Mortgage paid off", "Life expectancy" or
"Financial independence". A milestone is a date, an age, or a condition ("net worth at least ..."), or
several joined by AND and OR. Income, expenses and flows can start or end at a milestone, so moving
retirement moves everything tied to it. If a milestone is never reached, the plan says so.

## Flows and drawdown

**Flows** say what happens to money left over each year, in priority order: top up the emergency fund,
contribute to the 401(k) or workplace pension, fill the Roth IRA or ISA, then invest the rest. The order is the priority.

**Drawdown** says where money comes from when spending exceeds income. The default order is cash, then
taxable investments, then pensions, then tax-free accounts. You can reorder it, and set when each account
can be touched (a US 401(k) carries a 10% penalty before 59½ if you turn it on, and UK pensions cannot be
drawn before the minimum pension age, for example).

## Today's money versus future money

Every amount you enter says which it is.

- **Today's money** (real) is what the amount would buy at today's prices.
- **Future money** (nominal) is the actual number of pounds, dollars or euros in that year.

The engine calculates in future money and shows either. The toggle in the app's top bar, labelled
"Today's money" and "Future money", only changes the view, not the plan. Use today's money when you ask
whether you can afford a lifestyle, because a pound in 2060 is not a pound today.

## Amounts have a basis and a frequency

Every amount you enter has three parts: a value, a basis (`real` or `nominal`) and a frequency (such as
monthly or yearly). So "2,000 a month" can never be read as "2,000 a year". Money is entered in major
currency units (1234.56, not cents) and rates are percentages in the app (7%), stored as fractions (0.07).

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the frequency">
  A typo in the frequency changes a figure twelvefold. The assumption audit flags some mix-ups
  (such as mixing today's and future money), but not every one.
</Callout>

## Chance of success

One projection uses one fixed set of assumptions. Reality varies, so Glideworth also replays your plan many
times with different market histories and counts how often every year's spending was fully funded.
"85% chance of success" means 85 of 100 replays never ran short. It measures how fragile a plan is. It is
not a forecast. How it is computed is on [Monte Carlo and historical replay](/docs/methodology/monte-carlo/).
