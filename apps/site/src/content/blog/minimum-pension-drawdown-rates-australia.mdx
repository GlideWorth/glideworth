An account-based pension in Australia must pay you at least a minimum amount each financial year: 4% of the
balance under 65, 5% from 65 to 74, 6% from 75 to 79, 7% from 80 to 84, 9% from 85 to 89, 11% from 90 to 94 and
14% from 95. The percentage is applied to your balance on 1 July (or on the day the pension starts), and the
result is rounded to the nearest A$10. These minimum pension drawdown rates apply in 2026-27 and have been in
place since 2023-24, after four years at half these rates.

In return for paying at least the minimum, the fund pays no tax on the earnings and capital gains of the assets
behind a retirement-phase pension, and from 60 the payments to you from a taxed fund are tax-free. How much you can move into that
tax-free setting is limited by the transfer balance cap, A$2.1 million from 1 July 2026. This article covers the
rates, the calculation, what happens if the minimum is not paid, the tax treatment, and the caps.

## What is an account-based pension?

An account-based pension is a super income stream paid from an account held in your name: your super is moved
from accumulation into the pension, it stays invested, and the fund pays you from it at least once a year. To be
a pension under the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations, it must pay at least annually and pay a
minimum amount each year, and once it has started you cannot add contributions or rollovers to it
([ATO, Income stream (pension) rules and payments](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments), "About income streams" and "Minimum pension standards").
Apart from a transition to retirement pension (below), the standards set a minimum, not a maximum: you can take
more.

The ATO groups allocated pensions and market-linked pensions with account-based pensions when it describes the
minimum payment factors
([ATO, Payments from super](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/payments-from-super), "Minimum annual payments for super income streams").
Some other kinds of income stream work out the minimum from the purchase price instead of the balance (SIS
Regulations, Schedule 7, clause 2); this article is about the account-based kind.

## Minimum pension drawdown rates by age

The percentage factors are set in Schedule 7 of the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations 1994
([SIS Regulations, Compilation No. 158](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F1996B00580/2026-04-21/2026-04-21/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_2/document_2.html), Schedule 7, table)
and repeated by the ATO:

| Age | 2023-24 onwards, including 2026-27 | 2019-20 to 2022-23 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Under 65 | 4% | 2% |
| 65 to 74 | 5% | 2.5% |
| 75 to 79 | 6% | 3% |
| 80 to 84 | 7% | 3.5% |
| 85 to 89 | 9% | 4.5% |
| 90 to 94 | 11% | 5.5% |
| 95 or more | 14% | 7% |

Your age is your age on 1 July of the financial year, or on the day the pension starts if it starts during that
year (Schedule 7, clause 1).

### The temporary 50% reduction, 2019-20 to 2022-23

For the financial years starting 1 July 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2022, the minimum was half the normal amount
(Schedule 7, clause 4A). So the minimum pension drawdown for 2022 (the 2021-22 and 2022-23 years) was 2.5% of the
1 July balance at 65 to 74, and 2% under 65. The reduction was not extended past 2022-23, and the full rates have
applied since 1 July 2023 ([ATO, Payments from super](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/payments-from-super), "Temporarily reducing superannuation minimum payment amounts").
Earlier reductions applied in 2008-09 to 2010-11 (50%) and 2011-12 to 2012-13 (25%).

## How the minimum is worked out

The rules are in Schedule 7, clauses 1 to 5:

1. **Balance.** Take the pension account balance on 1 July of the financial year, or on the start day if the pension
   starts during the year. If the amount you would get by fully commuting the pension is higher, that amount is
   used instead.
2. **Factor.** Multiply by the percentage for your age.
3. **First year.** If the pension starts on a day other than 1 July, the minimum for that year is reduced in
   proportion to the days from the start day to 30 June, counting the start day. If it starts on or after 1 June, no
   payment is needed that year.
4. **Rounding.** Round to the nearest A$10; an amount ending in exactly A$5 is rounded up.

Partial commutations (lump sums taken from the pension) have not counted towards the minimum since 1 July 2017,
so if you want a withdrawal to count, take it as a pension payment
([ATO, Income stream (pension) rules and payments](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments), "Minimum pension standards").

### Worked example at age 67

<Callout type="note" title="Illustrative">
  Made-up round figures for 2026-27, to show the arithmetic. It is not a forecast or advice.
</Callout>

Jo is 67 on 1 July 2026 and has A$500,000 in an account-based pension on that day.

- Factor at 67: 5%.
- Minimum for 2026-27: 5% × A$500,000 = **A$25,000**, already a multiple of A$10. Paid monthly, that is about
  A$2,083 a month.

If instead Jo started the pension on 1 January 2027 with A$500,000:

- Full-year minimum: 5% × A$500,000 = A$25,000.
- Days from 1 January to 30 June 2027, counting both: 181. Days in 2026-27: 365.
- A$25,000 × 181 ÷ 365 = A$12,397.26, rounded to the nearest A$10: **A$12,400**.

For comparison, in 2022-23 the same A$500,000 at 67 would have needed only 2.5%, or A$12,500 for a full year.

The minimum is a floor, not a plan. If the portfolio earns less than the rate you draw, the balance falls; the
[how long will my money last calculator](/tools/how-long-will-my-money-last/) shows how long a balance lasts at
a given withdrawal and return.

## What happens if you do not take the minimum?

If the minimum is not paid in a year, the income stream is treated as having stopped at the start of that year for
income tax purposes. The payments for that year and later years are not super income stream benefits, the fund
cannot claim the tax exemption on the pension's earnings for that year or later years, and there are transfer
balance account consequences. To get a super income stream again, the old one has to end and a new one start, with
a new minimum worked out on the revalued balance
([ATO, Income stream (pension) rules and payments](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments), "When minimum pension standards aren't met").
The Commissioner has a limited discretion to let a pension continue in some circumstances. The ATO page is
written for self-managed funds, but the minimum is the same pension standard for every fund.

## Tax on account-based pensions

### Payments: tax-free from 60

A super income stream paid from a taxed fund (the "element taxed in the fund") is not assessable, not exempt
income if you are 60 or over: you pay no tax on it and do not count it in taxable income. Between preservation
age and 60, the taxable component is taxed at your marginal rates with a 15% tax offset
([ATO, Super income stream tax tables](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/super-income-stream-tax-tables), Table 15).
An untaxed element, which comes from a fund that has not paid tax on it, is taxed at marginal rates from 60 with a
10% tax offset (Table 16). Your fund's payment summary shows which elements you have.

### Earnings and capital gains: exempt in retirement phase

This is the Australian capital gains tax pension exemption people search for. Income a fund earns on assets that
support retirement-phase income streams is **exempt current pension income** (ECPI), and it is tax-exempt. It
covers both ordinary income, such as interest, dividends and rent, and statutory income, such as capital gains
([ATO, Exempt current pension income](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/smsf-administration-and-reporting/exempt-current-pension-income), "What is exempt current pension income?").

How it works depends on how the fund holds its assets. If assets are set aside solely to pay retirement-phase
pensions (segregated), all their income is exempt and capital gains and losses on them are ignored. If they are not
set aside, an actuary works out the share of the fund's income that is exempt, in proportion to the retirement-phase
balances, and that share is applied to the fund's income including its net capital gain (same page, "Segregated
method" and "Proportionate method"). Assessable contributions and non-arm's length income are never exempt. A fund
can only claim the exemption for a pension that meets the minimum payment standard, which is the link back to the
drawdown rates above.

The exemption belongs to the fund, not to you. Capital gains you make on assets you own personally, outside super,
are taxed as usual, with the 50% discount if you are an Australian resident and held the asset for at least 12
months ([ATO, CGT discount](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/cgt-discount));
the ATO notes that capital gains tax changes announced in the 2026-27 Budget do not apply to Tax Time 2026.

### Transition to retirement pensions are different

A transition to retirement income stream (TRIS), which lets you draw on part of your super while still working,
does **not** get the exemption. Since 1 July 2017 the assets supporting a TRIS earn taxable income at 15%. A TRIS
moves into the retirement phase, and its earnings become exempt, when you reach 65 or tell the fund you have met a
condition of release with no cashing restriction, such as retirement, permanent incapacity or terminal illness
([ATO, Transition to retirement income streams](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-and-super-professionals/for-superannuation-professionals/apra-regulated-funds/paying-benefits/income-streams/transition-to-retirement-income-streams)).
A TRIS has the same minimum as an account-based pension and also a maximum of 10% of the balance each year until
that condition is met ([ATO, Income stream (pension) rules and payments](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments), "Transition to retirement income streams").

## The transfer balance cap

The transfer balance cap is a lifetime limit on the total amount you can transfer into retirement-phase
income streams. It is what people mean by "2 million tax-free super": the general cap was A$2 million in 2025-26
and is **A$2.1 million** for 2026-27
([ATO, Transfer balance cap](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/transfer-balance-cap), Table 26).

| Year | General transfer balance cap |
| --- | ---: |
| 2017-18 to 2020-21 | A$1.6 million |
| 2021-22 and 2022-23 | A$1.7 million |
| 2023-24 and 2024-25 | A$1.9 million |
| 2025-26 | A$2.0 million |
| 2026-27 | A$2.1 million |

The cap is a limit on what you transfer in. Taking pension payments, even larger ones, does not create a debit in
your transfer balance account or free up cap space; commuting part of the pension does ([ATO, Excess transfer balance](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/super/withdrawing-and-using-your-super/retirement-withdrawal-lump-sum-or-income-stream/excess-transfer-balance), "What you can do").

### Your personal transfer balance cap

When you first start a retirement-phase income stream, your personal cap is the general cap at that time. The
general cap is indexed to the consumer price index in A$100,000 steps. If you have never used your full cap, your
personal cap rises by the unused percentage of each increase, based on the highest balance your transfer balance
account has ever had; if you have ever used it all, it does not rise
([ATO, Calculating your personal transfer balance cap](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/super/withdrawing-and-using-your-super/retirement-withdrawal-lump-sum-or-income-stream/calculating-your-personal-transfer-balance-cap)).
In the ATO's own example, someone who started a A$1 million pension in October 2025 with a A$2 million cap had
used 50%, so their cap rose by half of the A$100,000 increase, to A$2.05 million, on 1 July 2026. You can see your
personal cap in ATO online services through myGov.

If you go over your cap, you must commute the excess (with notional earnings on it) out of retirement phase, and you
pay excess transfer balance tax on those earnings: 15% the first time, 30% if it happens again
([ATO, Excess transfer balance](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/super/withdrawing-and-using-your-super/retirement-withdrawal-lump-sum-or-income-stream/excess-transfer-balance), "Excess transfer balance tax").
The excess can be moved back to an accumulation account or taken out of super as a lump sum (for a death benefit
income stream it must be taken out).

## Defined benefit income cap and capped defined benefit streams

Lifetime pensions, and lifetime annuities, life expectancy and market-linked pensions that existed before 1 July
2017, are **capped defined benefit income streams**. They count towards your transfer balance cap through a
"special value" (for a lifetime pension, 16 times the annual entitlement), but they cannot be commuted, so on their
own they never create an excess transfer balance
([ATO, Transfer balance cap – capped defined benefit income streams](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/super/withdrawing-and-using-your-super/retirement-withdrawal-lump-sum-or-income-stream/transfer-balance-cap-capped-defined-benefit-income-streams)).

Instead, the tax-free treatment from 60 is limited by the **defined benefit income cap**: A$131,250 for 2026-27,
up from A$125,000 in 2025-26 ([ATO, Transfer balance cap](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/transfer-balance-cap), Table 27).
If you are 60 or over and your capped defined benefit income is above the cap:

- for a taxed element and tax-free component, **50% of the excess** is included in your assessable income;
- for an untaxed element, the whole amount is assessable as usual, and the 10% tax offset applies only up to the
  cap, not to the excess.

For example, a 66-year-old with a A$150,000 lifetime pension paid entirely from a taxed element is A$18,750 over
the 2026-27 cap, so A$9,375 is added to their assessable income. If your pension is a defined benefit scheme such
as DFRDB, your fund can tell you whether it is a capped defined benefit income stream and which elements it pays;
the rules above then apply to it.

## Planning with this in Glideworth

Glideworth models super in two kinds of account, accumulation and pension phase. For a pension-phase account it
applies the minimum drawdown rate for the owner's age to the opening balance each year automatically, whether or not
the plan mentions it, and you can draw more. Payments from it are tax-free, and its earnings are untaxed, as for a
retirement-phase pension. The model uses age 60 both for access to super and for tax-free benefits. The
[Australia planning page](/planning/au/) lists what the model covers, and [tax estimates](/features/tax-estimates/)
explains how the figures are shown.

What it leaves out: the transfer balance cap is not modelled, so Glideworth does not stop you putting more than
A$2.1 million into a pension-phase account, and it does not apply excess transfer balance tax. Transition to
retirement pensions, the 10% maximum, the first-year pro-rating and A$10 rounding, untaxed elements and the
defined benefit income cap are not modelled either. To respect the cap, keep the amount in a pension-phase account within your personal cap and hold the rest
in an accumulation account.
