Yes. In the UK you pay tax on the State Pension: it counts as taxable income, along with any workplace or
private pension, earnings and savings income, and you pay income tax when your total income for the tax year
is more than your personal allowance, usually £12,570
([GOV.UK, Tax on your pension](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension)). The State Pension itself is paid with no
tax taken off, so the tax due on it is collected another way: through the tax code on your other pension or
job, or with a Simple Assessment bill from HMRC.

The full new State Pension in 2026/27 comes to just under the personal allowance, so a person whose only
income is the State Pension pays no income tax this year, and almost anything else they receive is taxed.
This article explains how that works, what changes from April 2027, when National Insurance stops (and why
income tax never does), and how tax on private pensions fits in, with a worked example.

## Is the State Pension taxable?

It is. GOV.UK lists "the State Pension you get (either the basic State Pension or the new State Pension)" and
the Additional State Pension among the income that is taxed, alongside private pensions, earnings, taxable
benefits, rent, savings interest and investments
([GOV.UK, Tax on your pension: what's taxed](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension/taxed)). What matters is the
total: "The amount of tax you pay depends on the total income you receive during the tax year"
([GOV.UK, Tax on your pension](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension)).

The difference from other income is how it is paid. "When you get your State Pension, it is not taxed before
you receive it" ([GOV.UK, Tax on your pension: how your tax is paid](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension/how-your-tax-is-paid)).
The Department for Work and Pensions pays it gross, and HMRC collects any tax due on it from somewhere else.

## How tax on the State Pension is collected

There are three routes, depending on what other income you have
([GOV.UK, how your tax is paid](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension/how-your-tax-is-paid)):

- **You also have a workplace or private pension, or a job.** Most pension providers and employers deduct
  income tax through Pay As You Earn (PAYE). HMRC changes your tax code so that this other income carries the
  tax on your State Pension as well. In practice the State Pension uses up most of your personal allowance, so
  your code gives the other payer only what is left. If you have several pensions or jobs, only one of them
  collects the State Pension tax, and each can have its own tax code. You get a P60 from each pension provider
  after the tax year ends.
- **The State Pension is your only income, or your other income is not taxed at source.** If your income is
  below your personal allowance you usually pay nothing. If it is above and you owe tax, HMRC sends you a
  Simple Assessment bill that says how much you owe and how to pay it.
- **You file a Self Assessment return.** If you already have to file one, for example because you are
  self-employed or have rental income, you include your State Pension and private pensions on the return.

If you think you have paid too much tax on a pension, GOV.UK has a refund checker
([Check how to claim a tax refund on your pension](https://www.gov.uk/claim-pension-tax-refund)).

## The personal allowance for 2026/27, and the frozen thresholds

For the tax year from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, the rates for England, Wales and Northern Ireland are
([GOV.UK, Income Tax rates and Personal Allowances](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates)):

| Band | Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Personal allowance | Up to £12,570 | 0% |
| Basic rate | £12,571 to £50,270 | 20% |
| Higher rate | £50,271 to £125,140 | 40% |
| Additional rate | Over £125,140 | 45% |

The personal allowance falls by £1 for every £2 of adjusted net income over £100,000, and is zero at £125,140.
Scotland has different bands and rates for non-savings income, including pensions.

These thresholds are frozen. The government has set the personal allowance at £12,570 and the higher-rate
threshold at £50,270 until 5 April 2031
([HMRC policy paper, Maintaining the Personal Allowance and the basic rate limit until 5 April 2031](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/maintaining-income-tax-and-equivalent-national-insurance-contributions-thresholds-until-5-april-2031/income-tax-maintaining-the-personal-allowance-and-the-basic-rate-limit-for-income-tax-and-equivalent-national-insurance-contributions-thresholds-unt)).
The State Pension, meanwhile, rises every April. That combination is why the question of tax on the State
Pension comes up more each year.

## How much is the State Pension, and how close is it to the allowance?

The full new State Pension is £241.30 a week in 2026/27
([GOV.UK, The new State Pension: what you'll get](https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension/what-youll-get)). Over
52 weeks that is £12,547.60, which leaves £22.40 of the £12,570 personal allowance. Your own amount can be
lower or higher, depending on your National Insurance record and any protected payment.

For 2027/28 the proposed full rate is £250.70 a week
([DWP, Proposed benefit and pension rates 2027 to 2028](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/benefit-and-pension-rates-2027-to-2028/proposed-benefit-and-pension-rates-2027-to-2028),
published 11 November 2026). Over 52 weeks that is £13,036.40, about £466 more than a personal allowance that
stays at £12,570. Without any other change, someone on the full new State Pension and nothing else would owe
about £93 of income tax that year.

## The Pension Tax Allowance from April 2027

Budget 2026 announced a measure for exactly this case. HMRC calls it a reduction in income tax for eligible
pensioners, or the Pension Tax Allowance
([HMRC policy paper, Reduction in Income Tax for eligible pensioners](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/income-tax-reduction-for-eligible-pensioners/reduction-in-income-tax-for-eligible-pensioners),
published 28 October 2026). As announced:

- **Who:** people at or above State Pension age. It depends on total income from all sources, not on whether
  you receive the State Pension, so people with a private pension or earnings can qualify.
- **How much:** a reduction in your income tax bill of 20% of the amount by which the full new State Pension
  exceeds the personal allowance. You get it in full if your total income is no more than the full new State
  Pension. It is a reduction in tax, not a cash payment, and it can never be more than the tax you owe.
- **Taper:** above that level it falls by £1 for every £3 of extra income until it reaches zero.
- **Figures for 2027/28:** the eligibility threshold below which the full amount applies is £13,037, and the
  allowance is fully removed at £13,319
  ([Budget 2026, Annex A: rates and allowances](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/budget-2026-overview-of-tax-legislation-and-rates-ootlar/annex-a-rates-and-allowances)).
  That makes the full reduction 20% × (£13,037 − £12,570) = £93.40.
- **When:** 2027/28, 2028/29 and 2029/30. It rises with the new State Pension for the rest of this Parliament;
  what happens after April 2030 will be considered separately.
- **Claiming:** none is needed. It is applied through tax codes for PAYE income and through the return for
  people who file Self Assessment, and HMRC says it is intended to stop small Simple Assessment bills for
  eligible pensioners.

It is not yet law: the policy paper says the legislation will be introduced in Finance Bill 2026-27.

The effect is that a pensioner whose total income is no more than the full new State Pension pays no income
tax in those years, even though that income is above the personal allowance. It does not raise the personal
allowance itself, and it is worth nothing to someone with income above £13,319 in 2027/28.

<Callout type="note" title="Not the pension annual allowance">
  People who search for a "pension tax allowance" sometimes mean the annual allowance: the most you can pay
  into pensions in a tax year with tax relief, £60,000 this tax year
  ([GOV.UK, Annual allowance](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-your-private-pension/annual-allowance)). That is a
  different rule from the Pension Tax Allowance described here.
</Callout>

## How much can a pensioner earn before paying tax?

In 2026/27 the answer is the same as for anyone else: total income up to the £12,570 personal allowance is
tax-free, and there is no extra allowance for age. If you receive the full new State Pension, that leaves
£22.40 for everything else, so almost every pound of a workplace pension, earnings or taxable interest is taxed.
(Interest can still fall within the savings allowances; that is a separate set of rules.)

From 2027/28, if the Pension Tax Allowance becomes law as announced, a person over State Pension age can have
total income up to £13,037 without paying income tax, with a partial reduction up to £13,319.

## Is there an age when you stop paying income tax?

No. There is no age at which income tax stops in the UK. Pensions, earnings and other income are taxed in the
same way at 70 or 90 as at 50, against the same personal allowance and bands. What does stop at State Pension
age is National Insurance.

## Do pensioners pay National Insurance?

Not on earnings after State Pension age:

- **Employees:** "If you're employed, you stop paying Class 1 National Insurance when you reach State Pension
  age" ([GOV.UK, National Insurance](https://www.gov.uk/national-insurance)).
- **Self-employed:** you stop paying Class 4 National Insurance from 6 April, the start of the tax year after
  you reach State Pension age (same source).

Income tax on those earnings still applies. National Insurance is paid on earnings from a job and on
self-employed profits; GOV.UK describes who pays in those terms, and the State Pension and private pensions are
neither. For comparison, the 2026/27 rates for people below State Pension age are 8% on employee
earnings between £242 and £967 a week and 2% above, and Class 4 at 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270 and
2% above ([GOV.UK, National Insurance rates](https://www.gov.uk/national-insurance/how-much-you-pay);
[GOV.UK, Self-employed National Insurance rates](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-national-insurance-rates)).

### What age do you stop paying National Insurance?

At your State Pension age, which depends on your date of birth
([DWP, State Pension age timetable](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/state-pension-age-timetable/state-pension-age-timetable)):

| Born | State Pension age |
|---|---|
| 6 October 1954 to 5 April 1960 | 66 |
| 6 April 1960 to 5 March 1961 | Between 66 years and 1 month and 66 years and 11 months |
| 6 March 1961 to 5 April 1977 | 67 |
| 6 April 1977 to 5 April 1978 | Between 67 and 68 (dates from 6 May 2044 to 6 March 2046) |
| 6 April 1978 or later | 68 |

The rise from 66 to 67 is happening now, between 2026 and 2028. The rise to 68 is set in current law for 2044
to 2046; the timetable is reviewed regularly and could change. You can check yours with the
[UK State Pension age calculator](/tools/uk-pension-age-calculator/).

## Tax on a workplace or private pension

Money taken from a defined contribution pension is taxed differently from the State Pension in two ways
([GOV.UK, Tax on your pension: what's tax-free](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-pension/tax-free)):

- **25% tax-free.** You can usually take up to 25% of the amount built up in any pension as a tax-free lump
  sum, up to a total of £268,275.
- **The rest is taxed as income.** Your provider takes tax off before paying you, through PAYE, using the tax
  code HMRC gives it. Large withdrawals in one year can push you into the higher rate.

### Emergency tax on a first flexible withdrawal

The first time you take a flexible payment from a pension, the provider often has no up-to-date tax code for
you. HMRC's guidance is that, unless you give the provider a P45 dated in the current tax year,
"the scheme administrator will use the emergency tax code on a month 1 basis against the first payment", and
HMRC then issues a code for later payments
([HMRC PAYE Manual, PAYE76170](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/paye-manual/paye76170)). A month 1
basis treats the payment as one month's income, as if the same amount would arrive every month, so a large
one-off withdrawal is often over-taxed.

You can reclaim the excess during the year. HMRC has three forms for flexibly accessed pensions: P55 (claim back tax on
a flexibly accessed pension overpayment), P53Z (when you have flexibly accessed all of your pension) and P50Z
(when you have also stopped work), and a checker that tells you which to use
([GOV.UK, Claim a tax refund on your pension by post](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/claim-a-tax-refund-on-your-pension-by-post)).
Using the wrong form can delay the refund. The [UK pension drawdown calculator](/tools/uk-pension-drawdown-calculator/)
shows how much of a withdrawal is tax-free and how much is taxed over a year.

## Worked example: State Pension plus a workplace pension, 2026/27

<Callout type="note" title="Illustrative">
  Made-up round figures, using the 2026/27 rates for England. This is not a forecast or advice.
</Callout>

Jan has reached State Pension age, lives in England and has no other income. In 2026/27 she receives:

- the full new State Pension, £241.30 × 52 = **£12,547.60**, paid with no tax deducted, and
- **£10,000** of taxable income from her workplace pension, paid through PAYE.

**Income tax for the year:**

- Total income: £12,547.60 + £10,000 = £22,547.60.
- Taxable income: £22,547.60 − £12,570 = £9,977.60, all inside the basic-rate band.
- Tax: £9,977.60 × 20% = **£1,995.52**.
- National Insurance: none, because Jan is over State Pension age and pension income does not attract it.

**How it is collected.** The State Pension uses all but £22.40 of Jan's allowance, so HMRC gives her
workplace pension a tax code with only that £22.40 left in it. The pension provider then deducts
(£10,000 − £22.40) × 20% = £1,995.52 over the year. The State Pension arrives in full, and the tax on it comes
out of the workplace pension.

**Net income:** £22,547.60 − £1,995.52 = **£20,552.08**.

**If the £10,000 were taken as uncrystallised lump sums instead,** 25% of each would be tax-free, so only
£7,500 would be taxable. Total taxable income would be £12,547.60 + £7,500 = £20,047.60, tax would be
(£20,047.60 − £12,570) × 20% = **£1,495.52**, and her net income would be £22,547.60 − £1,495.52 =
**£21,052.08**. (The first of those payments might be taxed on the emergency basis above and the excess
refunded later; the total for the year is the same.)

**If Jan had only the State Pension:** £12,547.60 is below £12,570, so she would pay no income tax in
2026/27. In 2027/28, on the proposed £250.70 a week, she would have £13,036.40, and tax before the Pension Tax
Allowance would be (£13,036.40 − £12,570) × 20% = £93.28. That is less than the £93.40 full reduction, so, if
the measure becomes law as announced, she would pay nothing.

## Planning with this in Glideworth

Glideworth's UK model includes the new State Pension (at £241.30 a week in 2026/27), your State Pension age
from your birth month, and income tax on the State Pension together with other pensions, earnings and savings,
at the rest-of-UK or Scottish rates. It stops National Insurance at State Pension age, applies the tax-free
share you set on each pension account (usually 25%) within the lump sum allowance, and applies the Pension Tax
Allowance as announced for 2027/28 to 2029/30, raising its threshold with prices after 2027/28 because later
amounts are not yet published. The details are on the [UK planning page](/planning/uk/), and the
[retirement tax calculator](/tools/retirement-tax-calculator/) gives a quick estimate.

Glideworth works out tax for each whole year, so it shows what you owe for the year rather than how PAYE
collects it: it does not model tax codes, emergency tax on a first withdrawal or the refund that follows.
Because it works in plan years, it also stops National Insurance in the year you reach State Pension age rather
than on the exact date. Its figures are [estimates](/features/tax-estimates/), not a tax return.
