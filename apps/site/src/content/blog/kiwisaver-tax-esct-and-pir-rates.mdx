KiwiSaver is taxed in three places. Your employer's contributions are taxed on the way in by **employer
superannuation contribution tax (ESCT)**, at a rate of 10.5% to 39% set by your salary plus employer
contributions in the previous tax year. The fund's earnings are taxed inside the fund at your **prescribed
investor rate (PIR)** of 10.5%, 17.5% or 28%. Your own contributions come out of pay that has already been taxed
in full. Withdrawals are not taxed: Inland Revenue says "you do not pay tax on any money you withdraw from your
KiwiSaver account"
([Inland Revenue, How your KiwiSaver income is taxed](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver/kiwisaver-individuals/taxing-kiwisaver-income)).

This article sets out the ESCT rates and how the threshold is worked out, the PIR rates and their income tests,
what happens when your PIR is wrong, the KiwiSaver contribution rates from 1 April 2026, and the ACC earners'
levy, with a worked example.

  and thresholds change; check Inland Revenue before you act. See the

</Callout>

## ESCT: employer superannuation contribution tax

ESCT is deducted from your employer's contributions to your KiwiSaver scheme or complying fund
([Inland Revenue, Employer superannuation contribution tax](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-income/employer-superannuation-contriibution-tax-esct)).
Your employer must contribute at least 3.5% of your gross salary or wages, deducts ESCT from that contribution
and pays the tax to Inland Revenue with its other payroll deductions
([IR335 Employer's guide, September 2026, page 26](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir335/ir335.pdf?modified=20260622210841)).
So the 3.5% your employer pays is a gross figure, and less than 3.5% of your pay reaches your account. Inland
Revenue puts it plainly: "Your employer must pay tax on their 3.5% contribution, so you may get less than 3.5 %
paid into your account"
([Inland Revenue, KiwiSaver benefits](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver/kiwisaver-individuals/kiwisaver-benefits)).

ESCT applies only to employer contributions. Money your employer takes from your own pay at your request is not
an employer contribution and has no ESCT
([Inland Revenue, ESCT](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-income/employer-superannuation-contriibution-tax-esct)).

### ESCT rates

These are the ESCT rate thresholds in Inland Revenue's current employer's guide
([IR335, September 2026, page 26](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir335/ir335.pdf?modified=20260622210841))
and in its PAYE tables for the year from 1 April 2026
([IR340, April 2026, page 4](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir340/ir340-apr-2026.pdf?modified=20260323203256)):

| Salary or wages plus gross employer contributions | ESCT rate |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| $0 to $18,720                                     | 10.5%     |
| $18,721 to $64,200                                | 17.5%     |
| $64,201 to $93,720                                | 30%       |
| $93,721 to $216,000                               | 33%       |
| $216,001 and over                                 | 39%       |

An employer paying into a defined benefit fund can choose a flat 39% instead (same source).

### How the ESCT threshold is worked out

- **The amount** is your total salary or wages from that employer **plus the gross employer contributions**, for
  the **previous tax year** (1 April to 31 March). Adding the employer contributions can push you over a
  threshold that your salary alone sits under.
- **The rate is set once a year**, at 1 April. If your pay changes during the year, the rate does not change
  until the next 1 April.
- **New employees**: if you have not worked for the employer for the whole previous year, the employer
  estimates your salary plus employer contributions for the year in which ESCT is being calculated and uses the
  rate for that estimate. IR335's example is a person who starts on 1 August on NZ$70,000 with 3.5% employer
  contributions: the employer counts the eight months to 31 March (NZ$48,300), applies 17.5%, and moves to 30%
  from the next 1 April, when the full-year figure is NZ$72,450.
- **Only this job counts.** The employer does not need to know what you earn in other jobs
  ([Inland Revenue, ESCT](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-income/employer-superannuation-contriibution-tax-esct)).
- **Whole dollars**: ESCT is worked out on each whole dollar of the contribution
  ([Inland Revenue, Deduct ESCT from each employer contribution](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-income/employer-superannuation-contriibution-tax-esct/deduct-esct-from-each-employer-contribution)).

If your employment agreement promises a **net** contribution (for example 5% after tax), the employer grosses it
up: ESCT = rate ÷ (1 − rate) × the amount paid to the fund. IR335's example is a 5% net contribution on
NZ$70,000 (NZ$3,500) at 30%, which needs NZ$1,500 of ESCT, so the gross contribution is NZ$5,000
([IR335, page 27](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir335/ir335.pdf?modified=20260622210841)).

### The option to treat employer contributions as salary

There is a second way. If your employer agrees, you can choose to have all or part of the employer contribution
included in your gross salary or wages and taxed under the PAYE rules instead of ESCT. You can change back at
any time. The money still goes into your fund, not your hand, and it has ACC earners' levy deducted like other
pay. IR335 notes that treating it as salary may affect Working for Families, the independent earner tax credit,
child support and student loan repayments
([IR335, pages 28 and 29](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir335/ir335.pdf?modified=20260622210841)).

## PIR: the prescribed investor rate on fund earnings

Most KiwiSaver schemes are portfolio investment entities (PIEs), and all the KiwiSaver default schemes are. A
PIE taxes your share of its earnings at the **prescribed investor rate** you give it. If your scheme is a
widely-held superannuation fund instead, its earnings are taxed at 28%
([Inland Revenue, How your KiwiSaver income is taxed](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver/kiwisaver-individuals/taxing-kiwisaver-income)).

### PIR rates and the income tests

For New Zealand tax resident individuals the PIR rates are 10.5%, 17.5% and 28%. Your PIR comes from two
tests on each of the **last two income years**: your taxable income (salary, wages and other income you would
put in a tax return), and your taxable income plus your PIE income. You use the lower rate that either year
gives you
([IR855 Information for resident individuals who invest in PIEs, April 2025, page 1](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir800---ir899/ir855/ir855.pdf?modified=20250331194632)):

| Taxable income        | Taxable income plus PIE income | In            | PIR   |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------- | ----- |
| $0 to $15,600         | $0 to $53,500                  | Either year   | 10.5% |
| $0 to $15,600         | $53,501 to $78,100             | Either year   | 17.5% |
| $15,601 to $53,500    | $0 to $78,100                  | Either year   | 17.5% |
| $53,501 or more       | Any amount                     | Each year     | 28%   |
| Any amount            | $78,101 or more                | Each year     | 28%   |

For the tax year ending 31 March 2027, the two years are those ending 31 March 2025 and 31 March 2026
([Inland Revenue, Find your prescribed investor rate](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/types-of-individual-income/portfolio-investment-entity-income-for-individuals/prescribed-investor-rates/find-my-prescribed-investor-rate)).
Because the test looks back, a year of high income keeps you on 28% only if both years were high: one lower
year is enough for the lower rate.

If you do not give your scheme a PIR, it uses the default of 28%
([Inland Revenue, New Zealand resident individuals' PIE income](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/types-of-individual-income/portfolio-investment-entity-income-for-individuals/nz-residents)).
You can tell the scheme your correct PIR at any time, and it may be able to back-date the change (IR855, page 2).

### What happens if your PIR is too low or too high

As a New Zealand tax resident with income from a multi-rate PIE, your annual income tax assessment includes a
separate PIE calculation. Inland Revenue works out the PIR you should have used for the full year and compares
the tax paid with the tax that should have been paid
([Inland Revenue, End-of-year PIE calculation and income tax assessments](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/types-of-individual-income/portfolio-investment-entity-income-for-individuals/end-of-year-pie-calculation)):

- **PIR too high**: you have a PIE credit. It reduces any income tax you owe, and any credit left over is
  refunded.
- **PIR too low**: you have a PIE debt, added to the income tax on your taxable income. If your residual income
  tax including the PIE debt is more than $5,000, you have to pay provisional tax the next year.
- **A losing year cuts both ways.** When the fund makes a loss, a PIR that is too high gives you a tax credit
  that is too large, and Inland Revenue's example shows the excess being clawed back as a PIE debt.

So a PIR that is set too high is no longer a cost you simply carry: the end-of-year calculation squares it up in
both directions. Telling your scheme the right rate still matters, because the difference only comes back after
the year ends.

### Can you have a 0% PIR?

Not by choice. Inland Revenue says resident individuals "cannot choose a PIR of 0%"
([Inland Revenue, New Zealand resident individuals' PIE income](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/types-of-individual-income/portfolio-investment-entity-income-for-individuals/nz-residents)).
A 0% rate applies in only two situations: a transitional resident (a new resident with the four-year temporary
tax exemption) who invests in a zero-rate PIE, and a person who withdraws their whole investment during a
quarter from a PIE that files quarterly, who may then owe tax at the end of the year (IR855, page 2).

## Your own contributions come from taxed pay

You choose to contribute 3.5%, 4%, 6%, 8% or 10% of your pay, and the default is 3.5%
([Inland Revenue, Employee contributions](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver/kiwisaver-individuals/employee-contributions)).
The percentage is of your before-tax pay, but it gives you no tax deduction: in Inland Revenue's PAYE tables
the PAYE on a given pay is the same whichever KiwiSaver rate you pick, and the KiwiSaver deduction is a
separate amount worked out on the same gross pay
([IR340, April 2026, pages 3 and 6](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir340/ir340-apr-2026.pdf?modified=20260323203256)).
In effect, you pay income tax on your contribution and then put it in.

## KiwiSaver contribution rates from 1 April 2026

Budget 2025 changed KiwiSaver contributions. Inland Revenue lists the changes
([Inland Revenue, KiwiSaver changes, updated 8 April 2026](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver-changes)):

- **From 1 April 2026** the default rate for both you and your employer rose from 3% to 3.5%. If you already
  contributed more than 3%, your rate did not change, but an employer paying 3% had to move to 3.5%.
- **From 1 April 2028** the default rate rises again to 4% for you and your employer.
- **Temporary rate reduction**: you can apply to stay at 3% for between 3 and 12 months at a time, as often as
  you like. Your employer can choose to match the lower rate.
- **16 and 17 year olds** qualify for employer contributions of 3.5% from 1 April 2026, if they meet the other
  eligibility rules, and qualify for the government contribution.
- **The government contribution**, from 1 July 2025, is 25 cents for each dollar you contribute in the year,
  up to NZ$260.72. You do not qualify if your taxable income is more than NZ$180,000 a year.

To get the full NZ$260.72 you need to contribute at least NZ$1,042.86 between 1 July and 30 June
([Inland Revenue, KiwiSaver benefits](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver/kiwisaver-individuals/kiwisaver-benefits)).
Your employer does not have to contribute if you are under 16, are eligible to withdraw your funds, are on a
savings suspension, or already get employer contributions to another complying scheme (same source).

## ACC earners' levy for 2026-27 and 2027-28

The ACC earners' levy is not a KiwiSaver tax, but it comes out of the same pay. It is a flat rate on earnings up
to a yearly maximum, and is included in PAYE
([Inland Revenue, ACC earners' levy rates](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/acc-clients-and-carers/acc-earners-levy-rates)):

| Tax year                           | Levy rate (incl. GST) | Maximum earnings | Maximum levy |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027      | 1.75%                 | $156,641         | $2,741.22    |
| 1 April 2027 to 31 March 2028      | 1.83%                 | $160,244         | $2,932.47    |

If you choose to have employer contributions treated as salary, they have the levy deducted too
([IR335, page 28](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir335/ir335.pdf?modified=20260622210841)).

## Worked example: ESCT on a salary

<Callout type="note" title="Illustrative">
  Made-up round figures to show the arithmetic with the rules above. Real payroll works per pay period and in
  whole dollars, so actual amounts differ by a few cents. It is not a forecast or advice.
</Callout>

Aroha earned NZ$80,000 in the 2025-26 tax year, and her employer contributed the old default of 3%.

1. **Threshold for 2026-27.** Salary NZ$80,000 plus employer contributions of 3% × NZ$80,000 = NZ$2,400 gives
   NZ$82,400. That is between $64,201 and $93,720, so her ESCT rate for 2026-27 is **30%**.
2. **Employer contribution in 2026-27.** Her salary is still NZ$80,000 and the default is now 3.5%, so the gross
   employer contribution is 3.5% × NZ$80,000 = **NZ$2,800**.
3. **ESCT.** 30% × NZ$2,800 = **NZ$840**, paid to Inland Revenue.
4. **Net employer contribution** into her account: NZ$2,800 − NZ$840 = **NZ$1,960**, or 2.45% of her salary.
5. **Her own contribution** at 3.5%: NZ$2,800, from taxed pay.
6. **Government contribution.** She contributes more than NZ$1,042.86 and her taxable income is under
   NZ$180,000, so it is the maximum **NZ$260.72** (counted over 1 July to 30 June).
7. **Into the account over the year:** NZ$2,800 + NZ$1,960 + NZ$260.72 = **NZ$5,020.72**.
8. **ACC earners' levy** on her pay: 1.75% × NZ$80,000 = **NZ$1,400**.

Then the fund's earnings are taxed inside the fund at her PIR. With taxable income above $53,500 in both of the
last two years, her PIR is 28%.

The threshold point matters most near a boundary. Someone who earned NZ$62,500 in 2025-26 with a 3% employer
contribution has NZ$62,500 + NZ$1,875 = NZ$64,375, just over $64,200, so their 2026-27 rate is 30%, not 17.5%,
even though their salary alone is below the threshold.

## Planning with this in Glideworth

Glideworth models KiwiSaver as a pension account: contributions come from taxed pay with no deduction, earnings
are taxed inside the fund, and withdrawals from age 65 are tax-free. As a simplification, it does not model ESCT
on employer contributions, PIR rates or the government contribution (see what is and is not modelled on the
[New Zealand page](/planning/nz/)). The account grows at the rate set for it (its own or the plan's), so if you
want the PIR in the plan, use a growth rate after the fund's tax.

To approximate ESCT, enter the employer contribution net of it. On the KiwiSaver contribution, set the employer
to add a share of your contribution that is already reduced by your ESCT rate: in the example above, 70% of your
contribution on the first 3.5% of pay (3.5% × 70% = 2.45%). You can add the government contribution as a fixed
yearly employer amount of up to NZ$260.72; the planner pays a fixed employer amount only while you have pay from
that job, which is a further simplification. The income tax and ACC earners' levy on your pay are estimated
for you; see [tax estimates](/features/tax-estimates/).
