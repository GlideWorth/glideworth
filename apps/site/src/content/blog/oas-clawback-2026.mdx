The OAS clawback, officially the Old Age Security recovery tax, takes back 15% of your net world income above a threshold. For
2026 income the threshold is **$95,323**: every dollar of net income above it costs 15 cents of OAS, until the whole pension
is gone at about $155,320 (age 65 to 74) or $161,320 (75 and over). The repayment for 2026 income is settled on your 2026
return and is also withheld from your OAS payments from July 2027 to June 2028
([Service Canada: recovery tax](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/recovery-tax.html)).

## What the OAS recovery tax is

Old Age Security is a monthly federal pension that starts from 65. It is income-tested at the top end: if your individual net world income is above the yearly threshold, you repay part or all of it
([Service Canada: OAS payment amounts](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/payments.html)).
In French it is the *impôt de récupération de la Sécurité de la vieillesse*, and the threshold is the *seuil minimal de
récupération de revenu*
([Service Canada, in French](https://www.canada.ca/fr/services/prestations/pensionspubliques/securite-vieillesse/impot-recuperation.html)).

The test is per person. A couple is not tested on joint income: each spouse's own net income is compared with the
threshold.

## OAS clawback threshold 2026 (and 2025)

Service Canada publishes the thresholds by income year, with the 12-month period whose payments they affect
([recovery tax](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/recovery-tax.html)):

| Income year | Payments affected | Clawback starts above | Fully clawed back, 65 to 74 | Fully clawed back, 75 and over |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2024 | July 2025 to June 2026 | $90,997 | $148,451 | $154,196 |
| 2025 | July 2026 to June 2027 | $93,454 | $152,062 | $157,923 |
| 2026 | July 2027 to June 2028 | $95,323 | $155,320 | $161,320 |

The upper limits depend on the maximum OAS pension, which changes every quarter. Service Canada marks the current year's
upper limits as estimates from January to September and final from October to December.

The rate is 15% of net world income above the threshold. The maximum OAS pension for October to December 2026 is $762.50 a
month from 65 to 74 and $838.75 a month from 75
([OAS payment amounts](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/payments.html)),
which is why the upper limit is higher for the older group.

## How the clawback is collected: the one-year lag

The clawback reaches you in two ways, and they are a year or more apart.

1. **On your tax return.** The repayment for a year is worked out on that year's return. It is calculated on the federal
   worksheet, added to the tax you owe, and deducted at line 23500. Any recovery tax already taken from your OAS cheques
   (box 22 of the T4A(OAS) slip) is claimed back at line 43700 as tax deducted
   ([CRA: line 23500](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/deductions-credits-expenses/line-23500-social-benefits-repayment.html)).
2. **Withheld from future payments.** Service Canada uses the net world income on your return to estimate your repayment for
   the following period, divides it into monthly instalments and takes them off your OAS payments, from July
   ([recovery tax](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/recovery-tax.html)).

So 2026 income sets the deductions from July 2027 to June 2028. The lag matters most after a one-off spike. A large RRIF
withdrawal or capital gain in 2026 triggers a repayment on the 2026 return, then reduced OAS cheques from July 2027 based
on that spike. If your 2027 income is lower, the excess withheld comes back when you file for 2027. If the deductions cause
financial hardship, you can ask the CRA to review them.

## The effective marginal rate it creates

In the clawback range, an extra dollar of net income costs 15 cents of OAS on top of ordinary income tax. Because the
repayment is deducted at line 23500, only 85 cents of that dollar ends up in taxable income. The combined rate is:

> effective marginal rate = 15% + 85% × your ordinary marginal rate

With the 2026 federal rate of 20.5% (taxable income between $58,523 and $117,045,
[CRA: tax rates](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/tax-rates-brackets/current-year.html)),
the federal part alone is 15% + 0.85 × 20.5% = **32.4%**, before provincial tax. If your combined federal and provincial rate
is 30%, the effective rate is 15% + 25.5% = 40.5%. This is the answer to a common question about the tax rate at
retirement in Canada: for incomes between the two thresholds, the rate you face is often higher than the bracket tables
suggest.

## A worked example

<Callout type="note" title="Illustrative">
  Made-up round figures for one person aged 70 in 2026, using the 2026 threshold and federal rate above. OAS is shown at the
  October to December 2026 rate for a full year to keep the arithmetic simple; the real amount changes each quarter. This is
  not a forecast or advice.
</Callout>

Net world income for 2026, before the repayment:

| Source | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| RRIF withdrawals | $60,000 |
| Workplace pension (life annuity) | $25,850 |
| CPP | $15,000 |
| OAS ($762.50 × 12) | $9,150 |
| **Total** | **$110,000** |

1. Income above the threshold: $110,000 − $95,323 = $14,677.
2. Recovery tax: 15% × $14,677 = **$2,201.55**.
3. OAS kept: $9,150 − $2,201.55 = $6,948.45.
4. Taxable income after the line 23500 deduction: $110,000 − $2,201.55 = $107,798.45.
5. An extra $1,000 of RRIF income would add $150 of recovery tax and $850 of taxable income, so $150 + 20.5% × $850 =
   $324.25 more federal tax and repayment, before provincial tax.

From July 2027, if nothing changes, Service Canada would withhold an estimate of the repayment from each monthly OAS
payment, based on this 2026 income.

## Ways people manage the OAS clawback

None of these is right for everyone, and each has trade-offs elsewhere in a plan.

### Pension income splitting

You can allocate up to 50% of eligible pension income to your spouse or common-law partner. From 65, eligible income
includes RRIF and LIF payments and life annuity payments from a pension plan; OAS, CPP and QPP cannot be split
([CRA: pension income splitting](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/pension-income-splitting.html)).
Both of you sign Form T1032 for each year you split, and the transferring spouse deducts the amount at line 21000
([CRA: line 21000](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/deductions-credits-expenses/line-21000-deduction-elected-split-pension-amount.html)).
Because the clawback is tested per person, moving income to the lower-income spouse can take the higher one below the
threshold.

<Callout type="note" title="Illustrative">
  Continuing the example: if the person above splits $15,000 of RRIF income with a spouse whose own income is $40,000, their
  net income falls to $95,000, below $95,323, and the $2,201.55 recovery tax disappears. The spouse's income rises to
  $55,000. Whether the household saves overall depends on both people's tax rates. Not a forecast or advice.
</Callout>

### TFSA withdrawals do not count

TFSA withdrawals are tax-free and do not affect federal income-tested benefits; the CRA names OAS and the Guaranteed Income
Supplement among them
([CRA: what is a TFSA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/tax-free-savings-account/what.html)).
Spending from a TFSA in a year when income would otherwise cross the threshold keeps net income down.

### Timing RRSP and RRIF withdrawals

RRIF withdrawals are taxable income, and the RRIF minimum rises with age (see
[RRIF and LIF withdrawal rates for 2026](/blog/rrif-and-lif-withdrawal-rates-2026/)). Some people draw more from an RRSP or
RRIF in lower-income years, before OAS or CPP start, so that the forced minimums later are smaller. Others avoid large
one-off withdrawals in a single year, because of the clawback and the lag above. Whether that helps depends on the tax paid
earlier against the tax and clawback avoided later.

### Deferring OAS to 70

You can delay OAS by up to five years. Each month of delay after 65 increases the payment by 0.6% (7.2% a year), up to 36%
at 70
([Service Canada: when to start OAS](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/when-start.html)).
Someone with high income in their late 60s, for example from work or a large RRIF, may lose most of OAS to the clawback in
those years anyway; deferring means a larger pension later, when income may be lower. The recovery tax still applies to
the larger pension if income stays above the threshold.

### The 10% increase at 75

Since July 2022, OAS is 10% higher from age 75
([OAS payment amounts](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/old-age-security/payments.html)). That is
why the full-clawback limit for 75 and over is higher: there is more pension to claw back, so it takes more income to lose
all of it. The starting threshold is the same for both age groups.

## Planning with this in Glideworth

Glideworth applies the OAS recovery tax in its Canadian tax estimate: 15% of net income above $95,323 for 2026, capped at
the OAS received, with the repayment deducted before the age amount and other income tests, and later years indexing the
threshold. It applies the recovery tax in the same year as the income, not with the one-year lag of real instalments, so a
spike shows up as tax that year rather than as lower OAS cheques later. Pension income splitting is optimised across federal
and provincial tax, and TFSA withdrawals are tax-free. See [retirement planning in Canada](/planning/ca/) for the full list
of what is modelled and left out.

OAS is an amount you enter with a start date. The planner does not add the 0.6% a month deferral increase or the 10%
increase at 75 for you, so enter the amount you expect to receive. GIS and its clawback are not modelled; you enter GIS as
an amount. To compare choices such as splitting or deferral, run the plan both ways and look at the
[tax estimates](/features/tax-estimates/) year by year.
