Glideworth separates two things that most spreadsheets mix together. **Current finances** are where you are
today: balances, property, debts. A **plan** is a scenario built on top of them: when you stop work, what you
spend, where surplus money goes and what has to be true for you to feel secure. You can keep as many plans as
you like on one set of finances, and update the finances without rewriting any of them.

## What you describe

**The household.** One person or a couple, with birth months, where each lives and a currency. Dependents
(children, people you support) are separate from the people, so a child's costs can start and stop on their own
dates. Adding a second person switches the household to couple mode.

**Accounts.** Cash and savings with a rate, taxable investments with a cost basis, tax-deferred and tax-free
retirement accounts, health and education accounts, crypto. Account types follow the country: 401(k)s, IRAs,
Roth accounts, HSAs and 529s in the US, ISAs, LISAs and pensions in the UK, TFSAs and RRSPs in Canada, super in
Australia, and the wrappers of the other countries the planner estimates tax for. Each account has an owner (you, your
partner or joint), a growth assumption, fees, and rules for when it can be touched.

**Real assets and debts.** Property, vehicles and other assets, owned outright or financed, with appreciation,
running costs, rental income and a planned sale or purchase. Debts compound and amortise, and can be paid down
by a flow you define.

**Income and expenses.** Salary, self-employment, bonuses, share vesting, defined-benefit and annuity income,
Social Security and state pensions, windfalls and inheritances; spending by type (housing, healthcare, education, travel, charity
and so on), marked essential or discretionary. Each item starts and ends on a date, an age, a year or a
milestone, can change faster or slower than inflation, and can repeat.

**Milestones.** Named moments the plan hangs things on: retirement, life expectancy, mortgage-free,
financial independence, or your own, defined as a date, an age, a condition such as "investments at least 25
times annual spending", or several joined with AND and OR. Tie an income or expense to a milestone and moving
the milestone moves everything attached to it. If a milestone is never reached, the plan says so.

**Flows.** What happens to money left over each year, in priority order: fill the emergency fund to a target,
take the employer match, fill the 401(k), Roth IRA or HSA (or an ISA in the UK), then invest the rest. The order is the priority.

## A worked illustration

The planner ships with synthetic sample households (no real data) so you can see the shape of a plan. This is
the structure of the US sample, a couple in Colorado, with amounts in today's money:

| Part | What the plan says |
| --- | --- |
| Household | Avery (born March 1984) and Jordan (born October 1986), married filing jointly, with one child |
| Accounts | High-yield savings, a joint taxable brokerage account, Avery's 401(k), Jordan's Roth IRA and a family HSA, plus a home with a mortgage and a car loan |
| Income | Avery's $132,000 salary and Jordan's $7,400 a month (part-time from 45) until the retirement milestone, then Social Security from 67 |
| Expenses | $5,200 a month of living costs, $9,000 a year of travel, healthcare from retirement and a replacement car in 2031 |
| Milestones | Retirement when Avery turns 57; life expectancy at 92 and 94; financial independence at 25 times spending; mortgage paid off |
| Flows | $23,000 a year into the 401(k), $7,000 into the Roth IRA, the emergency fund up to $36,000, then invest the rest |

Move the retirement milestone from 57 to 52 and both salaries end earlier, the 401(k) and Roth IRA contributions
stop earlier, healthcare costs start sooner, Social Security stays where it is, and the drawdown starts sooner.
Nothing else needed editing. That is the point
of building a plan from milestones rather than from dated rows.

## Several plans, one set of finances

Plans that start "now" read your current finances; plans can also freeze a snapshot of them. Update a balance
(by hand, from a CSV import, or from a script over the [API](/features/rest-api/)) and every live plan moves.
Each update can also record a progress snapshot, at most one automatic snapshot a day, so you can compare
reality with the plan over time (see [progress and check-ins](/features/progress-and-check-ins/)).

## What it does not do

- **No monthly resolution.** The engine steps by year (calendar, fiscal or rolling), prorating events by
  month inside a year. A crash in March and a recovery by December are not seen separately.
- **No bank connections.** You enter balances, import files or push them from a script. Glideworth never asks for
  bank credentials. See [importing](/features/import-and-export/).
- **No means-tested benefits.** Medicaid eligibility in the US and Universal Credit and Pension Credit in the UK
  are not modelled; neither are loss harvesting or specific-lot selling.
- **Some tax-specific items are simplified.** The municipal and Treasury bond-income split is not modelled, and
  each country's tax model lists what else it leaves out. See [tax estimates](/features/tax-estimates/).
- **Per-person privacy inside a household** is not modelled: everyone who can sign in to a household sees all
  of it.
- **Switching a couple to one person** keeps balances, but partner-owned accounts and income stay as they are
  until you reassign them.
