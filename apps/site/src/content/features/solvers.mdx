Most planners show you a result and leave you to find the change that fixes it by trial and error. Solvers turn
the question around. You name a target, such as a 90% chance of success, and the solver searches for the
answer: a spending figure in money, a retirement month, a savings amount.

## The solvers

- **Maximum spending:** the most you can spend, optionally limited to chosen expenses, while keeping the chance
  of success at your target.
- **Earliest retirement:** the earliest month a chosen milestone can happen, reported as a month and an age.
- **Required savings:** how much a chosen savings flow must be. If you name a flow that cannot be solved for, the
  tool says which flows can.
- **Required portfolio:** how large your invested balances must be, as a scale on the accounts you choose.
- **Safe withdrawal rate:** the highest constant withdrawal rate that works over a horizon of years (30 by default).
- **Goal seek:** any variable against any objective, within bounds you set, for questions the named solvers
  do not cover.
- **Tax-strategy ranking:** compares candidate tax strategies (see [withdrawal strategies](/features/withdrawal-strategies/)).

Every solver returns the same things: an answer written as a sentence, the supporting details, the answer at
neighbouring targets so you can see the trade-off, and the plan edits that would apply it. Nothing is applied
until you choose to.

## How one works

A solver runs the plan many times at different values of the thing it is solving for, each run a full
[Monte Carlo](/features/monte-carlo/) simulation, and narrows in on the value that meets the target.

Because each solver evaluation is a simulation, runs are jobs: they start in worker threads and report progress,
and you can wait for the result or poll for it. Settings trade accuracy for speed: fewer trials per evaluation
make a faster, noisier answer, and a cap on evaluations bounds the search. Runs use the seeded default draws, so
asking the same question twice gives the same answer.

## A worked illustration

On the synthetic UK sample household, asked at 90%, the spending solver answers in pounds a year what the
household could spend instead of its planned £31,600 (about £36,100 in the project's sample run), and offers the
edit as a change set. Asked about retirement at an 85% target, the earliest-retirement solver gives a month and an
age (about 56 and a half). These are illustrative results for a made-up household and one set of assumptions;
yours will differ.

The same questions work over MCP. A prompt named `how_much_can_i_spend` runs `solve_max_spending` and quotes the
figure per year; `can_i_retire_earlier` runs `solve_retirement_date`, explains the trade-off and offers a
branch. Over the REST API they are `POST /api/v1/plans/{id}/solve/max-spending`, `/earliest-retirement`,
`/required-savings`, `/required-portfolio`, `/safe-withdrawal-rate` and `/goal-seek`.

## Reading an answer

A solver result carries two statuses. The job status says whether the work finished. The solver's own outcome says
whether it converged on a value or ran out of evaluations first. A solver that stops at its evaluation cap gives
its best value so far, and says so.

An answer at 90% is not "safe". It is the point where, under your assumptions and the chosen market history,
nine of ten replays fund every year. Check the neighbouring targets, and read the
[failure analysis](/features/monte-carlo/) before acting.

## What it does not do

- **It answers inside your assumptions.** Garbage in, garbage out: if growth, inflation or tax settings are off,
  the answer is precisely wrong. The [assumption audit](/features/explanations-and-audit/) catches common mistakes.
- **It is not an optimiser of everything at once.** The tax-strategy ranking compares generated or custom
  candidates; it has no beam search or search-depth setting yet.
- **Randomised lifespan and care costs** are not inside the simulations it runs.
- **It does not give advice.** It tells you what your model says is possible.
- **No Flex versus No-Flex comparison table.** Risk-based guardrails are compared instead.
