A single projection assumes one future. Monte Carlo runs the same plan through many: each trial draws a sequence
of market returns, dividends and inflation, and the result is how often the plan meets its goals, the spread of
outcomes year by year, and an analysis of the trials that fail. "85% chance of success" means that in 85 of 100
replays every year's spending was fully funded. It measures how fragile the plan is. It is not a forecast.

## Methods

- **Historical restart** starts each trial at a random year of market history and continues forward.
- **Historical sequential** runs every start year once, so the trial count is set by the data.
- **Block bootstrap** resamples runs of consecutive years, which keeps some of history's streaks.
- **Normal** draws from a distribution fitted to history, with **lognormal** and **fat-tailed** (Student-t)
  options.
- Each series (stocks, bonds, inflation, dividends, crypto) can follow the method, come from a distribution, or
  stay fixed at the plan's assumption.
- Optional **exchange-rate risk** for plans that hold more than one currency (see [multi-currency](/features/multi-currency/)).

Only the market varies in a trial (and exchange rates, if you switch that on). Your income, spending, tax rules
and dates are the plan's, in every trial. A run can use up to 10,000 trials and defaults to 1,000.

## Seeded and repeatable

Unless you set a seed, a fixed default is used, so the same plan and settings give the same result, and "same
plan, change one number" compares like with like because both runs share the same random draws. The seed is
recorded in the result. Results are cached per plan version, and a large run is split across worker threads
and merged into exactly the result a single thread would give. Ask for a fresh random seed when you want one.

## What you learn

- The chance of success, and the range of balances and spending over time (the fan above).
- **Why it fails when it fails:** the years in which first shortfalls land, sequence-of-returns risk by how good
  or bad the early returns were, and failures before versus after retirement.
- **When milestones are reached** across trials, such as the distribution of retirement or financial
  independence dates.
- **Backtests:** replay the plan through every historical start year, and by-start-year results.
- Success can be defined strictly (every year funded), or as never depleted, liquid assets staying above a
  floor, or an end-of-plan floor.

## Worked illustration

This is the shape of a call, with illustrative figures. Over the REST API (`GW` is your server's address):

```bash
curl -s -X POST \
  "$GW/api/v1/plans/plan-main/monte-carlo?wait=60" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $KEY" \
  -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -d '{"settings":{"trials":2000}}'
# -> a job; numbers at links.result:
# successRate 0.86, p10/p50/p90, failures
```

A result reads like this: "86% of 2,000 trials fully funded every year. Of the 14% that failed, most first ran
short in the 2050s, and the weakest early-return quintile accounts for most of them." The follow-up is a
question the next feature answers: what to change. See [solvers](/features/solvers/) and
[explanations](/features/explanations-and-audit/).

## The market data

- **United States:** 1928 to 2025, the default for every country, including the UK.
- **United Kingdom:** 1983 to 2025, from openly licensed UK sources (inflation, Bank Rate, gilts), with US shares
  converted to sterling because no open UK share history exists. It is short and more optimistic than long UK
  history, which is why UK plans default to the US set; you can choose it in the settings.
- **Canada, Australia and the euro area** have no history of their own in the data, so they are tested against US history.
- **Crypto** has no history here and is sampled from a distribution.
- Datasets are not updated automatically. Where the data comes from, and under what terms, is recorded in the
  repository's data-licence file; see [open source](/open-source/#data).

<Callout type="warning" title="Past returns do not predict the future">
  A plan that works in every historical replay can still fail, and one that fails in some can work. The result is
  a stress test of your assumptions.
</Callout>

## What it does not do

- **Lifespan and care costs are not randomised.** You set life expectancy; a long-term-care scenario is a what-if,
  not a random shock. Randomised lifespan is on the [roadmap](/roadmap/).
- **Annual steps,** so intra-year sequence effects are not seen.
- **No published validation report.** The engine is compared with an independent historical calculator in a test,
  and seeded runs are reproducible, but a formal validation report is not published.
- **Plans do not re-run on every edit:** runs start on request.
